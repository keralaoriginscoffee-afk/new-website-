Kerala coffee harvest season.

Follow the coffee from ripening cherries to the tasting table, and understand what a new harvest really means.

A new coffee season starts long before a new bag reaches the shelf.

For a grower, it is a sequence of decisions: when to pick, what to separate, how to dry and which details to record. For a buyer, understanding that sequence makes conversations about availability and quality more useful. Kerala’s coffee harvest connects those two perspectives.

This guide explains the usual seasonal pattern and the work that follows. The calendar gives a starting point; a producer’s own observations tell us what is happening on a particular farm.

Read the calendar carefully

When is Kerala coffee harvested?

The Coffee Board of India describes the broad Indian harvest window as November to January for Arabica and December to February for Robusta. Wayanad’s district administration also places the region’s coffee harvest between December and February. These are general windows, not confirmed picking dates for every Kerala farm. [1] [2]

  • November–JanuaryThe broad Arabica harvesting window. Ask the producer which picking round and harvest their lot represents.
  • December–FebruaryThe broad Robusta harvesting window, including the seasonal pattern described for Wayanad.
  • After pickingProcessing, drying, preparation and evaluation still need to happen. The harvest month is not a ready-to-ship date.

Different coffees and locations need their own records. In particular, an Arabica or Robusta calendar should not be used to promise the timing of a specific Excelsa lot. Ask about that planting directly.

The season before the season

Flowering gives the harvest its beginning.

Kerala Agricultural University’s coffee guidance describes blossom-rain periods in February–March for Robusta and March–April for Arabica, followed by backing rains in April–May. This is a useful reminder that the picking season is connected to conditions many months earlier. [3]

For a farm record, a dated note about flowering is more useful than a general statement that the crop is “early” or “late”. Record the place, species and observations together. When comparing coffees across harvests, those notes can help frame the conversation without pretending that a calendar alone explains the flavour.

At the farm

Good records begin with the picking.

The Coffee Board’s post-harvest guidance emphasises ripe fruit, separation of unripe and overripe cherries, clean equipment and prompt handling. For wet processing, it advises pulping on the day of harvest and avoiding prolonged heaping. These basics matter before any elaborate processing description. [4]

A practical starting point is a simple lot sheet: farm or plot, species, picking date, cherry quantity, planned process and the person responsible. Keep that identity with the coffee as it moves through the farm. If batches are combined later, record that too.

Smaller, clearly identified trials can make feedback easier to interpret. Change one part of the process deliberately, keep the records and compare the resulting coffees. Discuss a trial with someone who understands the farm’s equipment and conditions before committing the whole harvest.

After the fruit is picked

Drying deserves its own attention.

Drying is a managed stage, not simply a number of days in the sun. TechnoServe’s wet-mill guide stresses regular turning, protection during drying, moisture checks and careful movement into storage. Its recommendations are written for wet mills; the appropriate routine still depends on the coffee, process and local conditions. [5]

For buyers, ask when drying finished, how it was monitored and how the lot has been stored. For producers, keep those details beside the picking and processing records. Our guide to washed, natural and honey coffee explains the main differences in what is being dried.

Once a sample is ready, label it so the tasting can be traced back to the coffee offered. Record who evaluated it, when and under what preparation. A promising early sample is the start of a conversation; it does not confirm quantity, delivery or a buyer’s final approval. The Kerala coffee buyer’s guide helps with those next questions.

A useful next step

Bring the coffee and its context.

Producers can begin by sharing their location, coffee type, intended process and the support they are looking for. Buyers can share their preferred preparation, quantity and destination. These details help us discuss relevant introductions and practical next steps.

Browse the producer directory, revisit a previous gathering or start a conversation. The purpose is to connect each harvest with the people who grew it, and to give their work the attention it deserves.
