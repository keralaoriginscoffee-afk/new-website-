Discover Kerala specialty coffee.

A guide to its regions and producers — and the details that connect a cup to its origin.

Follow the place.
Know the producer.
Taste the coffee.

A place name tells you where a coffee begins. The producer, the harvest and the care behind it tell you why it matters.

Kerala’s coffee is easier to understand when we look beyond the name of the state. Wayanad’s Robusta-growing farms, Arabica at estates in the Idukki high ranges, and the mixed coffee plantings of Nelliyampathy offer different starting points. Within each place, individual producers make decisions that deserve to be understood alongside the coffee itself.

The state is already an important producer. A 2024 study commissioned by the Government of Kerala, using 2023–24 estimates, put Kerala’s share at about one-fifth of India’s coffee production. It identified Wayanad, Idukki and Palakkad as the principal producing districts. Those figures describe the scale of coffee farming, not how much of the harvest qualifies as specialty coffee. [1]

For anyone discovering Kerala specialty coffee, the useful questions are more specific: who grew this coffee, where was it grown, how was it processed, and what distinguishes this particular lot? This guide introduces the regions, selected producers and information worth looking for, whether you are choosing a bag for home or evaluating coffee for a roastery.

Understanding the cup

What makes coffee specialty?

The Specialty Coffee Association’s definition centres on distinctive attributes that give a coffee additional value. That includes qualities experienced in the cup and information such as its origin, producer and production practices. Specialty coffee is therefore more than a geographic label or a single score. [2]

For readers, this starts with an identifiable lot, credible information about its production and an assessment of the actual coffee. A lot is a defined batch, which might be separated by harvest, farm plot or processing method. Knowing which batch is being described makes a tasting note or quality assessment much more useful.

A sense of place

The coffee regions of Kerala.

  • Wayanad Robusta & mixed farms
  • The high ranges Arabica & diverse plantings
  • Nelliyampathy Palakkad’s coffee hills

Wayanad: a closer look at Robusta.

Wayanad is a natural place to begin. Robusta is central to its coffee agriculture, and the district administration describes coffee grown alongside crops including pepper, arecanut and banana. This mixed-farming context is important: coffee can be one part of a producer’s livelihood and land management, rather than the only crop on the farm. [3]

Wayanad Robusta received geographical indication recognition in 2019. That recognition connects a named coffee to its geographical origin; it should not be read as a cupping score or a guarantee that every individual lot has the same quality. [4]

At Krishnagiri Estate in Wayanad, Gayathri M S is working with canephora coffee. Kerala Origins’ introduction to the estate describes more than 150 years of farming history and her work on its specialty coffee. It is a producer story worth following: an established farming background, with attention now directed towards how an individual coffee is prepared and presented. [14]

Look beyond the regional label. If a bag says only “Wayanad Robusta”, ask whether it comes from one farm or a collection of farms, and whether the seller can explain how that batch was prepared.

Idukki and the high range estates.

The Idukki high ranges provide another way into Kerala coffee. Windermere Estate, at Pothamedu near Munnar, describes an operating cardamom plantation that also produces small batches of Arabica. It is a useful reminder that the coffee story of this area extends beyond the tea plantations for which Munnar is widely recognised. [5]

Pampadumpara Estate and Kuruvinakunnel Estate also appear in Kerala Origins’ producer programme. The coffee recorded for Pampadumpara was washed Arabica, offering another starting point for readers interested in exploring Kerala-grown Arabica. Ask for current harvest details when following up with any producer. [14]

In the Vagamon hills, TerraVagamon Conservancy offers a distinct producer account. Its story, published by Bermito, describes coffee grown beneath a regenerating native canopy on land previously used for timber. The account identifies Arabica, Robusta and Excelsa as part of the planting, with coffee grown as an understory crop within the property’s wider restoration work. [6]

Together, these producers offer several routes into the high ranges: small-batch Arabica, washed lots and plantings that include less familiar species. Following a named producer makes it possible to return to their work over successive harvests and learn how their coffees develop.

Palakkad and the Nelliyampathy hills.

Palakkad is another of Kerala’s principal coffee-producing districts. In its Nelliyampathy hills, Poabs’ Seethargundu Estate grows both Arabica and Robusta within a multi-crop plantation that also includes tea and crops such as pepper and cardamom. Its own documentation describes coffee processing at the estate. [1] [7] [8]

Poabs’ published farming account describes organic and biodynamic methods. Its coffee documentation also distinguishes washed and other preparations. For someone exploring Nelliyampathy, these records offer a way to connect the place with farming choices and processing, then ask about the particular coffee available from the current harvest. [8]

These examples are a starting point, not a ranking or a complete directory of Kerala’s farms. A producer’s inclusion does not mean every coffee they grow has been evaluated by Kerala Origins.

More than one kind of coffee

Arabica, Robusta and Excelsa.

Kerala coffee should be approached with curiosity about species, without using the species name as a shortcut for quality. Robusta is commonly used for coffee from Coffea canephora. World Coffee Research highlights the species’ diversity and the scope for quality improvement; dismissing it before tasting a carefully prepared lot closes off part of the conversation. [9]

Arabica is also part of Kerala’s production, as the Windermere and Poabs examples show. Excelsa broadens that picture further through producers such as TerraVagamon. Current Kew research treats Excelsa as Coffea dewevrei, distinct from Coffea liberica; older material may use a different classification. [5] [6] [8] [10]

When choosing between coffees, start with the actual lot and your intended use. Ask how it was evaluated and roasted, and what the seller means by the tasting description. A species name is useful information, but it cannot replace a sample.

Reading the label

Understanding coffee processing.

Processing describes how the harvested coffee fruit is handled to produce the green coffee that will later be roasted. Three common terms help readers understand a label. [11]

  • WashedThe fruit skin and pulp are removed, followed by removal of the sticky mucilage before the parchment coffee is dried.
  • NaturalThe coffee is dried with the whole fruit around the seed, before the dried outer material is removed.
  • HoneyThe fruit is pulped and the coffee is dried with mucilage remaining. The term does not mean honey has been added. [11]

More detailed descriptions can identify a particular producer’s experiment. For example, Bermito’s TerraVagamon Excelsa listing describes a 120-hour anoxic fermentation followed by drying. That is information about a specified lot, not a definition of all Excelsa or all Vagamon coffee. [12]

For an experimental coffee, ask what was done, for how long, and how the coffee was subsequently dried. If fruit, other ingredients or selected cultures were introduced, that information should be disclosed. Clear records are more useful than treating an unusual process name as proof of a better cup.

For curious drinkers, roasters & buyers

What to ask before buying Kerala coffee.

A useful coffee description should make it possible to move from a broad origin to a particular purchase. For a bag of roasted beans, begin with the producer, harvest or lot information, process, roast date and recommended preparation. For green coffee sourcing, the conversation needs to go further.

  1. Identity and traceabilityWhich farm or producer group supplied the coffee, and is the sample linked to the lot being sold?
  2. Production detailsWhat species or variety is recorded, which harvest does it represent, and what processing steps were used?
  3. EvaluationWho assessed the coffee, when was it assessed, and are the tasting notes or scores tied to that particular sample?
  4. Availability and termsWhat quantity is available, in what form, and who is responsible for storage, delivery and the commercial agreement?

These questions do not need to become a barrier for smaller producers. They can be the basis of a practical conversation about what is already documented and what needs improving. For international buyers, an introduction or showcase is only the beginning; sample approval and the relevant shipping and commercial arrangements still need to be agreed.

Why we are here

The Kerala Origins movement.

Kerala Origins is a specialty coffee movement working to make Kerala’s producers and their coffees more visible. Established on 25 June 2026, it is a joint initiative co-founded by Bermito Coffee Roasters, TerraVagamon Conservancy, Silent Valley Estate and On Ground Farm. [14]

Our reason for coming together is straightforward. We want more Kerala coffees to be recognised through the people who grow them and the work that goes into individual lots. The aim is to help producers build relationships with roasters, brewers and buyers in India and internationally, while giving those buyers information they can use.

Bermito Coffee Roasters’ role includes roasting and evaluating samples so that producers and tasters can discuss the coffees together. Producers bring knowledge of their farms and processing; the roasting and cupping work helps them review the resulting coffee and consider what to test next. This practical exchange is part of how we want to support quality development. [14]

Our public programme has brought together producers including On Ground Farm, Silent Valley, TerraVagamon, Puliyarmala and Windermere. The Calicut event account records these participants in the July 2026 showcase, alongside guided cupping and conversations about farms and processing. It offers a record of the people and coffees introduced through the movement. [13]

The annual Kochi and Kozhikode gatherings are intended to connect this work with a wider audience. Beyond Kerala, our mission is to introduce these coffees through relevant festivals, tastings and buyer conversations. Producers interested in participating can contact Kerala Origins to discuss their coffee, sample preparation and the practical guidance they need. [14]

Start with a producer.
Then, a coffee.

Discovering Kerala specialty coffee does not require choosing one region or species as the best. Begin with a named producer, ask for the story of a particular harvest, and taste with an open mind.

Grow coffee, roast it, brew it or buy it? Start a conversation with Kerala Origins. Our purpose is to make those connections useful, with the producer’s identity and the coffee’s actual potential at the centre.
