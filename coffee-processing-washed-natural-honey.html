Practical coffee · From fruit to seed

Washed, natural or honey?

Follow the process. Understand the label. Ask better questions about the coffee in your cup.

Know the lot.
Understand the work.
Taste with curiosity.

A process name can open a conversation. It should never have to carry the whole story.

Pick up a bag of Kerala specialty coffee and you may find “washed”, “natural”, “honey” or a fermentation time on its label. These descriptions help explain decisions made after harvest. Read them alongside the producer, place, species and harvest, then return to the most useful question: what is this particular coffee like?

Coffee beans are seeds inside a fruit. Post-harvest processing separates those seeds from the surrounding material and prepares them for milling, storage and roasting. The Coffee Board of India emphasises ripe-fruit selection, clean equipment and prompt handling after picking. A distinctive process starts with attention to these everyday tasks. [2]

The useful distinction

What stays around the seed during drying?

This question makes the three familiar names easier to understand. The sticky layer beneath the fruit’s skin and pulp is called mucilage; parchment is a protective layer around the seed. TechnoServe’s processing guide distinguishes the methods by what is removed before drying. [1]

  • WashedThe fruit is pulped, then the mucilage is removed before the parchment coffee dries. Mucilage removal can involve fermentation, mechanical methods or a combination.
  • NaturalThe whole cherry is dried with the seed inside. The dried fruit layers are removed later during hulling.
  • HoneyThe fruit is pulped and the coffee dries with mucilage remaining. “Honey” describes this preparation; it does not mean honey was added.

You may also meet “pulped natural” or “semi-washed”. Terminology varies between regions and processors, so ask which layers were removed and when. A brief account of the actual steps is more useful than assuming every producer uses a label in exactly the same way. [1]

Read the label as information, not a ranking. Choosing washed over natural, or honey over washed, tells you a preference to explore. It does not establish which coffee is better.

Beyond one word

Where do anaerobic and anoxic fit?

Fermentation conditions are another part of the process description. Research distinguishes coffee fermented in open and closed systems, with different oxygen availability, fruit preparation and durations. A closed vessel and a longer time can change the outcome, but do not by themselves establish a better result. [3]

“Anoxic” refers to an oxygen-depleted environment; “anaerobic” describes processes occurring without oxygen. Coffee labels use these terms to describe oxygen-limited fermentation, but either word alone leaves practical details unanswered. Was the coffee whole fruit or pulped? Was water added? How was oxygen limited, and how was the coffee dried afterwards?

A 2025 Cenicafé study compared fruit and pulped-coffee fermentations under different oxygen conditions and times. Quality outcomes depended on the combination of conditions; longer fermentation was not uniformly beneficial. Treat that finding as a reason to ask for records and taste the lot, rather than a universal processing schedule for Kerala. [3]

For a producer, an achievable next step is a clear batch record: picking date, lot identity, process sequence and observations. Changes are easier to discuss when the sample and its record stay together.

Transparency in the cup

What does co-fermented mean?

The SCA distinguishes spontaneous fermentation, the addition of selected microbial starters, fermentation adjuncts such as fruit, and flavouring agents. These are different interventions. A fruit-like tasting note is not evidence that fruit was added, and a yeast starter is not the same thing as a flavouring. [4]

When a coffee is described as co-fermented or infused, ask what was introduced and at which stage. Our recommendation is straightforward: disclose additions plainly so buyers and drinkers can make an informed choice. The same openness should extend to a coffee processed without additions. A clear explanation gives the producer’s experiment proper context.

The less visible work

Drying and storage still matter.

Drying is a controlled reduction of moisture, not simply a pause between processing and sale. The International Trade Centre’s Coffee Guide explains that excessive speed or heat, and drying that is too slow, can damage quality in different ways. It recognises both sun and mechanical drying as viable when managed properly. [5]

The Coffee Board advises protection against re-wetting, appropriate dryness and clean storage away from dampness and materials that can contaminate coffee. For a buyer, ask how the lot was dried, how dryness was assessed and where it has been stored. “Raised beds” or a day count alone cannot answer those questions. [2]

Our Kerala coffee harvest-season guide follows the wider sequence from harvest to samples. Actual timing depends on the farm and lot; a harvest month is not a promise that green coffee is ready to dispatch.

Bring it back to taste

Compare the coffees, then compare the labels.

Try two or three coffees with the same brewing method and write down what you notice before checking the tasting notes. If you want to explore processing specifically, ask for coffees from a comparable origin and harvest. Even then, variety, ripeness, roasting and brewing can differ, so describe your experience without claiming the process caused every difference.

The SCA’s definition of specialty coffee includes both sensory characteristics and information about its origin and production. Understanding how a coffee was made adds context to tasting; the name on the bag does not replace it. [6]

Keep the conversation practical

Five questions for your next coffee.

  1. Which coffee is this?Ask for the producer, region, species or variety, harvest and lot identity.
  2. What happened after picking?Ask for the sequence, including when the fruit or mucilage was removed.
  3. What was added?Ask about water, selected cultures, fruit and flavouring ingredients separately.
  4. How was it dried and stored?Ask for measurements and handling details relevant to the lot.
  5. Can we taste the actual lot?Match the sample to the coffee you intend to buy, and discuss its intended use.

For producers taking their first steps, these questions can become a simple record sheet rather than a barrier to participation. Talk with Kerala Origins about the support you need. For roasters and buyers, our Kerala coffee buyer’s guide explains how to turn that conversation into a useful enquiry.

Give the process a story.
Give the producer credit.

When the label connects to real decisions and an identifiable coffee, it becomes easier to recognise the work behind the cup. Explore the regions and producers of Kerala specialty coffee, and keep asking questions worth sharing.
